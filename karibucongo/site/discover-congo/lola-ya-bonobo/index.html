Thirty kilometres from the centre of Kinshasa, a 30-hectare patch of protected forest is home to bonobos that were taken from the wild as babies and rescued from the illegal bushmeat and pet trades. Lola ya Bonobo, which means "paradise for bonobos" in Lingala, is where they are raised, healed and, for some, prepared to return to the forest.

It is the only sanctuary in the world dedicated exclusively to orphaned bonobos, and bonobos live nowhere else on Earth except the Democratic Republic of the Congo. This guide explains what the sanctuary is, why it matters, how rehabilitation works step by step, and how to plan a visit.

What Lola ya Bonobo is

A rescue centre, a classroom and a forest refuge in one place.

Lola ya Bonobo is run by the Congolese non-profit Les Amis des Bonobos du Congo (ABC), founded in 1994 by Belgian-born conservationist Claudine André. The idea began when orphaned bonobos started turning up for sale on the streets of Kinshasa. The sanctuary has been at its present site, beside the Petites Chutes de la Lukaya waterfalls just south of the Kimwenza suburb, since 2002.

The numbers inside the fence change as new orphans arrive and groups move on. Published figures have ranged from 52 bonobos in 2007 to more than 70 by 2017. Alongside the animal care, the sanctuary educates large numbers of Congolese visitors, including schoolchildren, about the bonobo as part of the country's own natural heritage.

Why bonobos need a sanctuary

One country, one forest block, a shrinking range.

Bonobos are found only in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The Congo River separates them from chimpanzees and gorillas, and their whole range is an island-like block of forest south of the river, roughly a fifth of the country. They were not recognised as a species separate from chimpanzees until the 1930s, which makes them the last of the great apes to be described scientifically.

The IUCN Red List classifies the bonobo as Endangered. Nobody knows exactly how many remain, because only about 30% of the historic range has ever been surveyed. Estimates suggest a minimum of 15,000 to 20,000 animals, with the overall trend heading down. A 2013 range-wide study found that only 28% of the range was suitable bonobo habitat, and just over a quarter of that suitable habitat lay inside protected areas.

The pressures are habitat loss, a growing and moving human population, years of conflict and instability, and hunting. Bonobos are often described as trusting around people, which makes them easy to catch. Hunters kill the mothers for bushmeat and sell the infants as pets, and those infants are the orphans that end up at Lola.

From rescue to release: how rehabilitation works

Bonobos cannot simply be dropped back into the forest. Recovery is slow and relational.

Many orphans arrive frightened, underweight and traumatised, often after seeing their mothers killed. The sanctuary's answer is to replace what they lost, starting with a person.

  1. Rescue and arrival

    Orphans arrive after being rescued from the bushmeat and pet trades, typically as very young infants. The first priority is warmth, milk and constant care.

  2. A surrogate human mother

    Each newcomer is assigned a human caregiver who acts as its mother. She bathes the young bonobo daily, rubs its fur with oil, feeds it from a bottle and holds it for comfort. This physical affection is what helps an infant overcome trauma and survive.

  3. Nursery, play and learning

    Footage from the sanctuary shows young bonobos playing, climbing and sharing food around their caregivers. Learning to be a bonobo happens among other bonobos, so this time together matters as much as feeding.

  4. Joining a group

    Caregivers keep looking after each bonobo until it is ready to join others. Integration is gradual, and building a stable troupe can take several years.

  5. Release into protected forest

    Troupes judged ready move on to Ekolo ya Bonobo ("land of the bonobos"), a protected forest in Equateur Province, far from Kinshasa, to complete their return to the wild. The first release succeeded in 2009, and the sanctuary also supports local communities there with health and school supplies.

Why surrogate mothers? A bonobo infant depends on its mother for years, for milk, warmth, protection and social learning. Without a substitute, an orphan can fail to thrive. The people at Lola fill that role until the animal can live as part of a bonobo group.

Life at the sanctuary

Forest, enclosures and a lot of play.

The sanctuary sits in protected forest, with wire-fenced enclosures where groups of older bonobos forage and rest. Younger bonobos spend time with caregivers in a nursery area, where climbing frames, ropes, old tyres and a large trampoline give them room to run, climb and bounce. Living in a natural environment is part of how rescued animals heal and thrive.

Watching the groups, the resemblance to people is hard to miss: sitting in pairs, handling food carefully, glancing at one another. The sanctuary's own video ends on that thought, that in a bonobo's expressive eyes we see a reflection of ourselves, and a responsibility to protect what remains.

The people behind it

A long-running Congolese effort.

Claudine André came to Congo from Belgium at a young age. After a chance meeting with a bonobo at the Kinshasa zoo, she dedicated her life to the species and built a place where orphans could be cared for properly. Today her daughter, Fanny Minesi, is general director of ABC, overseeing the sanctuary, the release site at Ekolo ya Bonobo, the education programmes and visitor services.

The caregivers who work with the bonobos every day are the heart of the programme, supported by staff that include graduates in biology and ecology from the University of Kinshasa. Lola ya Bonobo belongs to the Pan African Sanctuary Alliance, the network of primate sanctuaries across the continent. Visits and donations help fund the daily cost of feeding and caring for the animals.

Getting there

The sanctuary is about 30 km (roughly 20 miles) from central Kinshasa, next to the Lukaya waterfalls in the hills south of the city. Allow time for Kinshasa traffic and set aside around three to four hours in total. The sanctuary can arrange round-trip transport from central Kinshasa for $150 per vehicle, which carries up to three people. Transport and lunch need to be requested at least a week ahead.

Lunch and overnight stays

A lunch package ($50 per person) includes a meal, two drinks and entry. For a longer visit, a small eco-lodge offers overnight stays with a private tour and the chance to join a caregiver at feeding time. Space is limited, so email the sanctuary with your dates and party size to ask about rates.

Visitor rules. There is no physical contact with the bonobos. They share many illnesses with humans, so a cold can reach them, and nursery orphans do best with limited human contact. Phones are fine for personal photos, but professional camera or video equipment needs prior authorisation, ideally requested at least four weeks ahead. Many visitors prefer the dry season, roughly June to September, when road access is easier.

Frequently asked questions

What does Lola ya Bonobo mean?

It means "paradise for bonobos" in Lingala, the main language of Kinshasa. It is both the sanctuary's name and the name people use for the organisation behind it.

Where do bonobos live in the wild?

Only in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, in the humid lowland forests south of the Congo River. They do not occur naturally in any other country.

Can you touch or hold the bonobos?

No. Visitors keep their distance. Bonobos can catch human illnesses, and the sanctuary's orphans do best with limited human contact, so only trained caregivers handle them.

When is Lola ya Bonobo open?

Day visits run Tuesday to Sunday, with guided tours at 10:00, 11:30, 13:00 and 14:30. The sanctuary is closed on Mondays. Check with the sanctuary before you travel, because schedules can change.

How much does it cost?

The sanctuary lists $10 for adults, $5 for ages 12 to 17, and free entry for children under 12. Lunch ($50 per person) and round-trip transport from central Kinshasa ($150 per vehicle) are optional extras that need booking about a week ahead.

Are bonobos the same as chimpanzees?

No. They are separate species in the same genus, Pan. Bonobos are found only south of the Congo River and are known for societies in which strong bonds among females play a central role.

Are the bonobos ever returned to the wild?

Yes, some are. Groups judged ready are moved to Ekolo ya Bonobo, a protected forest in Equateur Province, to complete their return to the wild. The first release succeeded in 2009.
